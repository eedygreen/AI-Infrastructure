DESIGN NOTES AND REFLECTION REPORT
Beavers Paper Company (Munder Difflin): multi-agent ordering system

This report has three parts: (1) the agent workflow diagram, beavers_agents_detailed.mermaid,
explained in words, with the roles of the agents and the decisions behind the architecture;
(2) the evaluation results in test_results.csv and the strengths they show; (3) drawbacks and
suggested improvements.

1. THE AGENT WORKFLOW DIAGRAM: THE ROLES AND THE DECISIONS BEHIND IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------
A customer sends an inventory question ("do you have this?"), a quote request ("what would this
cost?") or an order ("sell me this"). Five agents work together to understand it, check stock,
price it with a bulk discount, buy more stock when there is a shortfall and delivery can still
be on time, record the sale, and reply. Separately, and without ever delaying a customer, the
system keeps the warehouse stocked. An "item" means one product the customer wants, with its
quantity ("500 cardstock").

The five agents and their roles

Each tool belongs to exactly one agent, so the responsibilities do not overlap. A task is only
handed the tools it needs: a stock check never gets a tool that writes.

CustomerSupportAgent  Understands the customer's words: maps products to the catalog, sorts the
                      request (order / quote / inventory), runs the customer loop.
                      lookup_catalog_item: catalog data only
OrchestratorAgent     Plans, delegates, decides what may happen next, writes the reply.
                      Plain code: no model and no tools.
InventoryAgent        Stock, supplier delivery dates and buying stock. Never records sales.
                      check_item_stock: get_stock_level, get_supplier_delivery_date
                      restock_for_order, restock_for_replenishment: get_cash_balance,
                        get_supplier_delivery_date, create_transaction
                      find_low_stock: get_all_inventory
QuoteAgent            Prices requests and applies discounts within limits. Never touches stock
                      or sales.
                      search_history: search_quote_history
                      get_pricing_context: generate_financial_report
                      price_line: pricing rules only
OrderAgent            Records the sale. The only agent that writes sales.
                      finalize_sale: create_transaction

How a request flows (the diagram, step by step)

The customer loop: the request goes to the CustomerSupportAgent, then to the orchestrator. The
loop repeats for follow-ups until the customer is satisfied or 3 iterations have passed, then
it escalates to a person. It carries the request id, the iteration and earlier results, and a
placed order is never replaced.

 1. Plan. An order needs a stock check and a quote first, and a quote needs a stock check, so
    the plan adds them even when the customer only asked to order.
 2. Phase 1, look-ups done at the same time. The InventoryAgent checks stock for each item and
    asks the supplier for a delivery date (ETA) for any shortfall. The QuoteAgent looks up
    similar past quotes. Neither needs the other and neither changes anything. If the
    supplier does not answer, the ETA is "unknown" and in-stock items are unaffected.
 3. Phase 2, pricing. If stock is known, the QuoteAgent prices each item. It reads the sales
    report (internal only) so that top sellers get smaller discounts, then applies a bulk
    discount that never goes below the margin floor (cost plus 10%). If stock could not be
    read the system does not guess: the quote is reported as unavailable.
 4. Phase 3, changes, only after a check. Only when an order was asked for. For each item:
        in stock                                      ship
        short, ETA in time, quote OK                  buy stock if cash stays above the cash
                                                      floor (the minimum cash to keep), then
                                                      ship
        ETA too late or unknown, restock denied,      not placed, nothing changed, and the
        or no quote                                   reason is given to the customer
    The sale is recorded after the stock is checked again just before the write (it may have
    changed since Phase 1), under a write lock.
 5. Reply. The orchestrator writes it from fixed templates and from results the tools stored.
    It points out anything that could not be done, and why, and removes internal figures
    (cash, margins, supplier cost).
 6. Background replenishment. After the reply has gone, the InventoryAgent finds items below
    their reorder point (their minimum stock level) and tops them up if cash allows;
    otherwise it skips the item and flags it for a person to review. It can never change what
    the customer was told or charged.

Rules everywhere: every database and supplier call has a time limit and a few retries. A
look-up may be repeated freely. A change is repeated only with its idempotency key, a unique
label built from the run, request, item and quantity, so a repeat is recognised and happens
once. Every failure ends in a safe retry or a reported reason, never in a silent change.

Why the architecture looks this way

The decisions follow from four questions asked of every step. Does it need language
understanding (a model), or must it always hold (code)? Does it change the books (then it
needs a check and must be safe to repeat)? Does the customer have to wait for it (if not, move
it to the background)? Who needs which tools (give each task the least access)?

 1. Five specialised agents, not one agent with every tool. One agent that can price, buy stock
    and record sales can make a wrong sale from a single wrong tool call. Splitting by
    responsibility gives each task only the access it needs. Five is the project's limit, and
    each role maps to a real job: understanding, coordinating, stock, price, sale.
 2. A deterministic orchestrator, not a model. The order of the work and the checks (stock,
    deadline, cash) are rules, not judgments. As ordinary code they can be tested and cannot
    be talked into a sale. Models are used only where language is needed: reading the message
    and choosing tools and a discount within limits. The cost: it cannot adapt to a
    situation nobody planned for.
 3. Guards in the tools, not in the prompts. A prompt is a request; code is a guarantee. The
    cash floor, the stock re-check, the discount cap and the margin floor are enforced in
    code. If the sales report is unavailable, every discount cap is halved (fail closed).
 4. Look-ups in parallel, changes only after checks. The parallel steps change nothing, so
    they save time without risk. The one phase that changes the books runs only after the
    earlier phases succeeded.
 5. Every write is safe to repeat, so a retry or a repeated call never records a sale twice.
 6. Replenishment runs after the reply, so it can never delay a customer or change an outcome.
 7. Fixed templates for replies, so internal figures cannot leak and every refusal has a reason.

2. EVALUATION RESULTS (test_results.csv)
----------------------------------------
All 20 requests in quote_requests_sample.csv were run in date order on a freshly seeded
database. Each row holds the cash and inventory value after a request, and the reply.

    Requests answered                        19 of 20 (request 15: the model call failed)
    Items sold or quoted                     54: 44 confirmed, 10 not fulfilled
    Requests naming products we do not sell  4 (2, 3, 13, 20)
    Cash changed after                       18 of 19 steps: $45,139.97 after request 1,
                                             $49,377.72 after request 20
    Confirmed order value                    $18,817.72 (74% of it in request 3)

This is a single run of a model that is not deterministic, so how much outcomes vary is not
measured. The strengths it shows:

 - It fulfils what it can and says what it cannot. Request 14: 500 cardstock is confirmed,
   while 5,000 A4 and 2,000 poster paper are refused with the earliest delivery date
   (2025-04-16). Six requests (10, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19) went this way.
 - An unknown product does not block the rest. Request 20 reports "tickets" as not sold and
   still sells the flyers and the poster paper.
 - Decisions are explained: 17 items show a bulk discount, 36 say there is none because the
   item must be restocked, 27 say delivery includes restocking time, and all 10 refusals give
   the reason and the earliest date.
 - Safe replies: no cash, margins, supplier cost or error text.
 - It fails honestly. When the model call failed (request 15) the customer was told there was
   a temporary problem, and nothing changed: cash and inventory match those after request 14.

3. DRAWBACKS AND FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS
-----------------------------------
Three drawbacks, each answered by the improvement with the same number.

 D1. Product matching is loose and can contradict itself. Requests 2, 3 and 13 say a product
     could not be matched and then sell a different item in its place. Request 3 sold 15,000
     A4: the 10,000 asked for plus the 5,000 A3 that the same reply says it could not match.
     It also sold 250,000 sheets of standard copy paper ($13,000) for "500 reams of printer
     paper", also reported as not matched. Sizes such as A3 are dropped silently.
 F1. Accountable matching: a catalog table with sizes, synonyms and units (packets, boxes,
     reams). The reply names the product it matched, asks the customer to confirm whenever a
     detail was dropped, never lists a product as both sold and unmatched, and checks the
     merged total of an item, not only its parts.

 D2. A missed deadline is only refused. When the supplier cannot deliver by the customer's
     date, the system reports the earliest date but offers nothing else (10 of 54 items).
 F2. Offer a way forward: ship what is in stock now, or offer the later date for acceptance,
     so that most refusals become a choice for the customer.

 D3. The customer has little control. Quote and order are one step, so a price is never
     approved before the sale, and a placed order cannot be changed except by a person.
 F3. A quote-acceptance step for large or heavily discounted orders (send the quotation first,
     place the order in the next loop iteration, only after the customer confirms), and an
     order lifecycle (quoted, confirmed, amended, cancelled, delivered) with amend and cancel
     as guarded paths and a defined hand-off to a person.
